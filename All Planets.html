Solar System Field Guide

Eight Worlds.
One Star.

Explore all eight planets, from tiny, cratered Mercury to distant, windy Neptune. Each world has its own strange weather, landscape and story.

Before you launch

The planets in order from the Sun are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. The first four are rocky worlds. Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants; Uranus and Neptune are ice giants. The numbers below are approximate, and moon counts can change as new moons are confirmed.

Diameter means approximate equatorial diameter; distance is average distance from the Sun. Temperatures are broad averages or typical estimates, and conditions can vary dramatically across a planet.

☿

1. Mercury

The smallest planet — and the closest to the Sun

Mercury is a small, rocky world covered in impact craters. It has almost no atmosphere to hold heat, so its surface swings between extreme daytime heat and nighttime cold.

Planet facts

  • Mercury is the smallest planet in the Solar System.
  • It is the closest planet to the Sun, but it is not the hottest planet.
  • Its surface is heavily cratered, rather like Earth's Moon.
  • Mercury has a very thin exosphere rather than a substantial atmosphere.
  • Because it has almost no air to spread heat around, the surface temperature varies enormously between day and night.
  • A solar day on Mercury — sunrise to sunrise — lasts about 176 Earth days.
  • Mercury completes one orbit around the Sun in about 88 Earth days.
  • It has a large iron-rich core and a global magnetic field, though much weaker than Earth's.
  • Water ice can persist in permanently shadowed craters near its poles.
  • Mercury has no moons and no ring system.

2. Venus

A world with a crushing atmosphere

Venus is similar to Earth in size, but its environment is radically different. A thick carbon-dioxide atmosphere traps heat in an intense greenhouse effect, making Venus the hottest planet.

Planet facts

  • Venus is the hottest planet in the Solar System, even though Mercury is closer to the Sun.
  • Its atmosphere is mostly carbon dioxide, with clouds containing droplets of sulfuric acid.
  • The atmospheric pressure at the surface is about 92 times Earth's sea-level pressure.
  • Surface temperatures are around 465°C — hot enough to melt lead.
  • Venus rotates in the opposite direction to most planets; on Venus, the Sun would generally rise in the west.
  • One rotation takes about 243 Earth days, longer than its 225-day year.
  • Venus has no Earth-like oceans or confirmed surface liquid water.
  • Its surface has mountains, vast volcanic plains and many volcanoes.
  • Thick clouds hide the surface from ordinary visible-light views, so radar has been used to map it.
  • Venus has no moons and no ring system.

3. Earth

Our home — the only world known to host life

Earth has liquid-water oceans on its surface, a protective atmosphere and a magnetic field. So far, it is the only place where life is known to exist.

Planet facts

  • Earth is the third planet from the Sun and the largest of the four rocky planets.
  • About 71% of its surface is covered by water.
  • Its atmosphere is mostly nitrogen and oxygen.
  • Earth's atmosphere and magnetic field help protect the surface from some hazards in space.
  • Earth's seasons happen mainly because its axis is tilted, not because it is much closer to the Sun in summer.
  • Earth takes about 365.25 days to orbit the Sun; the extra quarter-day is why leap years are needed.
  • One rotation relative to the stars takes about 23 hours 56 minutes; the familiar solar day averages 24 hours.
  • Earth's Moon helps drive ocean tides and stabilizes Earth's axial tilt over long timescales.
  • Earth's outer core is liquid metal, and its motion helps generate the planet's magnetic field.
  • Earth is not a perfect sphere: its rotation makes it slightly wider around the equator.

4. Mars

The Red Planet

Mars is a cold, rocky desert world. Iron minerals in its dust and rocks oxidize — much like rust — giving the planet its familiar reddish colour.

Planet facts

  • Mars is the fourth planet from the Sun and about half Earth's diameter.
  • A Martian day, called a sol, is about 24 hours 39 minutes long.
  • A year on Mars lasts about 687 Earth days.
  • Mars has the largest volcano known in the Solar System: Olympus Mons.
  • Valles Marineris is a vast canyon system stretching thousands of kilometres.
  • The atmosphere is thin and mostly carbon dioxide, so liquid water is unstable across most of the surface today.
  • Mars has two small moons, Phobos and Deimos.
  • Polar caps contain water ice and seasonal carbon-dioxide ice.
  • Robotic orbiters, landers and rovers have explored Mars; scientists investigate whether it once had environments suitable for microbial life.
  • Mars has seasons because its axis is tilted, and its dust storms can sometimes grow to cover much of the planet.

5. Jupiter

King of the planets

Jupiter is the largest planet in the Solar System. It is a giant world made mostly of hydrogen and helium, with powerful storms and a huge family of moons.

Planet facts

  • Jupiter is so massive that all the other planets combined still have less mass than Jupiter.
  • It is a gas giant with no ordinary solid surface to stand on.
  • Its Great Red Spot is a gigantic storm that has been observed for centuries, although its size and appearance change.
  • Jupiter rotates very quickly: one day is about 9 hours 56 minutes.
  • Despite its fast rotation, Jupiter takes almost 12 Earth years to orbit the Sun.
  • Jupiter has a faint ring system made mainly of dust.
  • The four largest moons — Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto — are called the Galilean moons.
  • Ganymede is larger than the planet Mercury.
  • Europa has a global ocean beneath its icy crust and is a major target in the search for potentially habitable environments.
  • Jupiter has an enormous magnetic field and intense radiation belts.

6. Saturn

The spectacular ringed planet

Saturn is a gas giant famous for its wide, bright rings. The rings are made mostly of countless pieces of water ice, mixed with rocky material and dust.

Planet facts

  • Saturn is the second-largest planet in the Solar System.
  • Its rings extend a huge distance from the planet but are remarkably thin compared with their width.
  • Saturn's average density is lower than water's, although that does not mean it could float in any realistic ocean.
  • A Saturn day is only about 10.7 Earth hours long.
  • One Saturn year lasts about 29.5 Earth years.
  • Saturn's north pole has a long-lasting, six-sided jet-stream pattern known as the hexagon.
  • Titan, Saturn's largest moon, has a thick nitrogen-rich atmosphere and lakes and seas of liquid methane and ethane.
  • Enceladus sprays plumes of water vapour and ice grains from its south polar region; evidence indicates a global ocean beneath its icy shell.
  • Saturn's rings are divided into named sections, with gaps shaped partly by gravitational interactions with moons.
  • Saturn has no solid surface like Earth; deeper down, pressure and temperature rise enormously.

7. Uranus

The planet that rolls around the Sun

Uranus is an ice giant with a blue-green colour caused by methane in its atmosphere. Its extreme axial tilt makes its seasons especially unusual.

Planet facts

  • Uranus rotates on its side: its axis is tilted by about 98 degrees.
  • Because of this tilt, each pole experiences decades of sunlight followed by decades of darkness during its long orbit.
  • Uranus is classified as an ice giant, not a gas giant like Jupiter and Saturn.
  • Methane in the atmosphere absorbs red light, helping give Uranus its blue-green appearance.
  • Uranus has a faint ring system, discovered in 1977.
  • Its year lasts about 84 Earth years.
  • One rotation takes about 17 hours, though its sideways orientation makes the idea of a day unusual.
  • Uranus is extremely cold in its upper atmosphere and has very little internally generated heat escaping compared with other giant planets.
  • Its atmosphere contains hydrogen and helium as well as methane; deeper inside, water-, ammonia- and methane-rich materials are thought to be important.
  • Voyager 2 is the only spacecraft to have visited Uranus closely, flying past in 1986.

8. Neptune

The distant, windy world

Neptune is the farthest recognised planet from the Sun. It is a dark, cold ice giant with powerful winds and storms in its atmosphere.

Planet facts

  • Neptune is the eighth planet from the Sun.
  • It takes about 165 Earth years to complete one orbit, so it has only completed a little more than one orbit since its discovery in 1846.
  • Neptune has some of the fastest winds measured in the Solar System, reaching well over 1,000 km/h in some conditions.
  • Its blue appearance is associated with methane and other atmospheric effects; the exact colour is more subtle than some enhanced images suggest.
  • Neptune is an ice giant, with hydrogen, helium and methane above a hot, dense interior rich in heavier materials.
  • Neptune has a faint ring system, including clumpy arcs.
  • Triton, its largest moon, orbits in the opposite direction to Neptune's rotation, suggesting it was captured from elsewhere.
  • Triton is extremely cold and has nitrogen ice on its surface; Voyager 2 observed geyser-like plumes there.
  • Neptune was discovered in 1846 after mathematical calculations predicted where it should be found.
  • Voyager 2 made the only close flyby of Neptune, in 1989.

Quick comparison

  • Smallest planet: Mercury.
  • Largest planet: Jupiter.
  • Hottest planet: Venus.
  • Planet with the most famous rings: Saturn — though all four giant planets have rings.
  • Planet with the shortest year: Mercury.
  • Planet with the longest year: Neptune.
  • Only planet known to support life: Earth.
  • Two ice giants: Uranus and Neptune.
